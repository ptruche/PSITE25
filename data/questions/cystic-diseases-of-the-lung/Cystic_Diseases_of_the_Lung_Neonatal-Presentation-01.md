A newborn is delivered at term after prenatal detection of a large left thoracic mass. Within minutes of birth, the infant develops severe respiratory distress and rightward mediastinal shift is noted on chest radiograph. Despite intubation and ventilator support, oxygenation remains poor and echocardiography reveals right ventricular strain and pulmonary hypertension. Which of the following best describes the expected neonatal presentation of large congenital lung malformations?

<!-- EXPLANATION -->

**Large congenital lung malformations (CLMs)**, such as **congenital pulmonary airway malformation (CPAM)**, **bronchopulmonary sequestration (BPS)**, or **congenital lobar emphysema (CLE)**, can produce **significant mass effect and air trapping**, leading to **respiratory compromise at birth**. Although the majority of cases are asymptomatic, approximately **10–15%** of prenatally diagnosed lesions cause **symptoms in the neonatal period**.

---

### **1) Mechanisms of Respiratory Distress**
Large CLMs **compress adjacent normal lung and mediastinal structures**, resulting in:  
- **Decreased alveolar ventilation** → hypoxia and hypercarbia.  
- **Mediastinal shift** → impaired venous return and decreased cardiac output.  
- **Right ventricular strain** → pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure.  

| **Mechanism** | **Effect** |
|----------------|------------|
| Mass effect | Mediastinal shift, compression of normal lung |
| Air trapping (CPAM, CLE) | Overinflation → increased intrathoracic pressure |
| Pulmonary hypertension | Right heart strain, hypoxemia |
| Cyst rupture | Pneumothorax |

---

### **2) Clinical Spectrum of Neonatal Disease**
The severity of respiratory compromise varies:  
- **Mild:** transient tachypnea or mild desaturation.  
- **Moderate:** persistent tachypnea requiring oxygen or CPAP.  
- **Severe:** **respiratory failure** requiring mechanical ventilation.  
- **Critical:** refractory hypoxemia with **pulmonary hypertension**, necessitating **extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO)**.  

In rare instances, a **ruptured cyst within a CPAM** can cause **spontaneous pneumothorax** and rapid clinical deterioration.

---

### **3) Distinguishing Lesion Types**
- **CPAM:** cystic, air-filled mass; may rupture → pneumothorax.  
- **BPS:** solid echogenic mass with systemic arterial supply; can cause mediastinal compression.  
- **CLE:** hyperinflated lobe due to bronchial obstruction; progressive air trapping and mediastinal shift.  

| **Lesion** | **Key Pathophysiology** | **Typical Neonatal Presentation** |
|-------------|--------------------------|----------------------------------|
| CPAM | Cystic overgrowth of bronchioles; air trapping | Respiratory distress; rare pneumothorax |
| BPS | Systemic arterial supply; no bronchial communication | Mass effect, respiratory failure |
| CLE | Air trapping due to bronchial obstruction | Rapid respiratory distress postnatally |

---

### **4) Severe Neonatal Outcomes**
- **10–15%** of fetuses with CPAM or BPS develop **respiratory symptoms at birth**.  
- In rare, severe cases, **pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure** occur.  
- **ECMO** may be lifesaving for **refractory respiratory failure** with **severe pulmonary hypertension**.

---

### **5) Management Principles**
- **Immediate stabilization**: airway control, gentle ventilation (avoid barotrauma).  
- **Avoid overdistention** in air-trapping lesions (CLE, CPAM).  
- **Surgical resection** (lobectomy) once stabilized or after ECMO recovery if required.  
- **Prognosis:** excellent with timely diagnosis and management.
