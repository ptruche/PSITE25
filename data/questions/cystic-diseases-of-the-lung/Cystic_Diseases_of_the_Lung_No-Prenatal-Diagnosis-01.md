A term infant with no prenatal diagnosis presents at 3 weeks of age with respiratory distress and a chest radiograph showing a right lower lobe cystic lesion with an air-fluid level. Following a course of antibiotics for presumed pneumonia, the lesion persists. What is the most appropriate next step in management and diagnostic evaluation?

<!-- EXPLANATION -->

### **Evaluation of Congenital Lung Lesions Discovered Postnatally**

When congenital pulmonary malformations are identified **without a prenatal diagnosis**, the evaluation depends on:  
- **How the lesion was discovered** (incidental vs symptomatic),  
- **Patient age and clinical status**, and  
- **Information needed to guide management and surgical planning**.

---

### **1) Incidental Lesions**
- If a cystic lung lesion is **incidentally discovered** (e.g., imaging for unrelated reasons such as trauma), the diagnosis must be confirmed.  
- **Axial imaging with multidetector CT with angiography (MDCTA)** is typically required to:  
  - Characterize the lesion’s anatomy,  
  - Determine the **presence of systemic vascular supply**, and  
  - Plan for **surgical resection** if indicated.  
- **MRI** may substitute for MDCTA when **iodinated contrast is contraindicated** or when differentiation from **posterior mediastinal lesions** (e.g., neuroenteric cysts) is necessary.  
- A **V/Q scan** may assist in diagnosing **congenital lobar emphysema (CLE)**, though this is **rarely needed**.

| **Scenario** | **Preferred Imaging** | **Purpose** |
|---------------|-----------------------|--------------|
| Incidental finding | MDCTA | Define anatomy, vascular supply |
| Contrast contraindicated | MRI | Clarify origin (lung vs mediastinum) |
| Suspected CLE | V/Q scan (rare) | Confirm decreased ventilation-perfusion |

---

### **2) Symptomatic Lesions**
- Infants presenting with **pneumonia**, **pneumatocele**, or **lung abscess** may harbor an **underlying congenital lesion**.  
- Distinguishing **acquired** from **congenital** pathology is challenging during acute infection.  
- **Stepwise management:**  
  1. **Treat infection first** with appropriate antimicrobials.  
  2. Once clinically improved, perform **MDCTA** to evaluate for a **persistent cystic lesion**.  
  3. Persistent lesions warrant **surgical resection** to prevent recurrence or malignant degeneration.

---

### **3) Infectious Disease Considerations**
- A complex cystic lesion unresponsive to first-line antibiotics should prompt evaluation for **fungal** or **mycobacterial** disease.  
  - Obtain **cultures** from sputum or abscess fluid.  
  - Consider **tuberculin skin testing** for **mycobacterial infection**.  
  - **Echinococcal disease** should be suspected when multiple thin-walled cysts are seen; **serum antibody testing** is diagnostic.  
- Nutritional status should be assessed in children with chronic infections.  

| **Possible Infectious Etiology** | **Diagnostic Test** |
|----------------------------------|----------------------|
| Mycobacterial infection | Tuberculin skin test, cultures |
| Fungal infection | Sputum/abscess culture |
| Parasitic infection (Echinococcus) | Serologic antibody testing |

---

### **4) Genetic Testing**
- **DICER1 mutation testing** is indicated for family members of patients diagnosed with **pleuropulmonary blastoma (PPB)**.  
- Other genetic analyses are **not routinely indicated**.

---

### **5) Summary Table**

| **Clinical Context** | **Next Step** | **Goal** |
|----------------------|---------------|-----------|
| Symptomatic pneumonia/abscess | Treat infection, then MDCTA | Define congenital lesion |
| Incidental lesion | MDCTA | Characterize and plan surgery |
| Contrast allergy | MRI | Differentiate from mediastinal mass |
| Suspected infection | Cultures, serology | Identify etiology |

---

### **Key Takeaways**
- **MDCTA after infection resolution** is the **gold standard** for diagnosis and surgical planning.  
- **Infection control precedes surgery.**  
- **V/Q scan** and **MRI** are reserved for select cases.  
- **Infectious and genetic differentials** must be considered when the lesion persists or presents atypically.
