A 4-month-old infant with a prenatally diagnosed congenital pulmonary airway malformation (CPAM) is being observed expectantly. The parents ask about the long-term outlook if surgery is delayed and what to expect after resection. Which of the following statements best summarizes current evidence?

<!-- EXPLANATION -->

### **Outcomes of Congenital Pulmonary Malformations: Operative vs Nonoperative Management**

#### **1) Nonoperative Management**
- Due to increased **antenatal detection**, many asymptomatic lesions are initially managed conservatively.
- Studies show variable outcomes:
  - **UK series:** Only **5%** of 58 patients developed symptoms over 5 years (mostly pneumonia or shunt-related).  
  - Other centers report **up to 86%** developing symptoms, often recurrent infection or respiratory distress.
- **Cancer risk** is *extremely low but not zero*, with rare reports of malignant transformation (e.g., pleuropulmonary blastoma).
- Many lesions decrease in size over time, but **resolution is not guaranteed**.
- **Observation** requires long-term follow-up imaging and prompt surgery for symptomatic progression.

---

#### **2) Postoperative Pulmonary Function**
- **Lung function after lobectomy** is typically **normal** in children due to **compensatory lung growth**.
- Early resection (infancy) coincides with the **alveolar growth phase**, optimizing long-term pulmonary capacity.
- Extent of resection correlates with outcome:
  - **Lobectomy:** Excellent long-term function.
  - **Multilobar or pneumonectomy:** May reduce total lung volume, but compensation still occurs.

---

#### **3) Long-Term Morbidity After Thoracotomy**
- **Modern muscle-sparing techniques** preserve chest wall function and reduce complications such as:
  - Scapular winging  
  - Scoliosis  
- No strong evidence that **thoracoscopy** yields significantly better long-term results compared with **muscle-sparing thoracotomy**.
- The historical morbidity seen with older open techniques (rib resection, muscle division) is largely eliminated in contemporary practice.

---

#### **4) Key Takeaways**

| **Aspect** | **Summary** |
|-------------|--------------|
| **Nonoperative management** | May be safe in select cases, but up to 86% develop symptoms |
| **Cancer risk** | Extremely rare but not zero |
| **Post-lobectomy function** | Normal due to compensatory growth |
| **Modern open surgery** | Low long-term morbidity with muscle-sparing technique |
| **Thoracoscopic vs open** | No proven superiority; both yield excellent outcomes |

---

**Incorrect answers:**  
- **A:** Many lesions persist or develop symptoms.  
- **B:** Lung function is typically normal after lobectomy.  
- **D:** Muscle-sparing thoracotomy prevents, not causes, chest wall deformities.  
- **E:** No evidence supports thoracoscopic inferiority.  

**Correct answer: C — Most prenatally diagnosed lung lesions remain asymptomatic, but some develop infection or shunting, and early resection allows for compensatory lung growth with normal long-term function.**
