A 22-week fetus is found on ultrasound to have a small left lower lobe echogenic lung lesion consistent with a congenital pulmonary airway malformation (CPAM). No other anomalies are noted, and there is no hydrops. What additional prenatal evaluation is most appropriate?

<!-- EXPLANATION -->

### **Evaluation of Fetal Lung Lesions During Prenatal Diagnosis**

Prenatal diagnosis of **congenital lung malformations** has become increasingly common with the widespread use of **high-resolution fetal ultrasonography**. The diagnostic and management strategy depends primarily on the **size of the lesion** and the **presence or absence of associated anomalies or hydrops**.

---

### **1) Routine Evaluation**
- **>90%** of fetuses with small CPAMs or bronchopulmonary sequestrations (BPS) do **not** develop hydrops.  
- These cases are best managed with **close ultrasound surveillance** through the remainder of pregnancy.  
- **Fetal surgery** for microcystic lesions is **rarely required** today due to effective **maternal steroid therapy**.  
- The primary goal of serial ultrasound is to monitor:  
  - Lesion growth and echogenicity.  
  - Development of mediastinal shift or hydrops.  
  - Overall fetal well-being to guide **delivery planning**.

---

### **2) Additional Diagnostic Tests**

| **Associated Findings** | **Recommended Test** | **Purpose** |
|--------------------------|----------------------|--------------|
| Solitary lung lesion, no other abnormalities | None beyond serial US | Low risk; no indication for genetic testing |
| Lung lesion + other anatomic anomaly | **Amniocentesis** | Genetic screening for chromosomal or syndromic anomalies |
| Concern for hydrops or cardiac dysfunction | **Fetal echocardiography** | Evaluate for structural or functional cardiac disease |
| Unclear diagnosis or suspected CDH | **Fetal MRI** | Differentiate CPAM/BPS from diaphragmatic hernia; assess lung volume and risk of pulmonary hypoplasia |

**Key points:**  
- **Amniocentesis is not routinely indicated** unless **additional anomalies** are present.  
- **Fetal echocardiography** is useful in the context of **hydrops or suspected heart failure**.  
- **Fetal MRI** is **selective**, not routine—reserved for large lesions, equivocal ultrasound findings, or when **prenatal intervention** is being considered.

---

### **3) Role of Imaging Modalities**
- **Fetal ultrasound:** mainstay for ongoing monitoring of lesion size and hydrops development.  
- **Fetal MRI:** clarifies anatomy when ultrasound is inconclusive, differentiates CPAM from congenital diaphragmatic hernia (CDH), and estimates normal lung volume to predict risk of pulmonary hypoplasia.  
- **Cost-effectiveness:** MRI adds limited prognostic value for small or moderate lesions, but is important before **fetal surgery** or **severe space-occupying lesions**.

---

### **4) Management Principles**
- **Maternal corticosteroids** are indicated for large **microcystic CPAMs** to reduce lesion size and improve fetal survival.  
- **Hydropic lesions** require close multidisciplinary coordination involving maternal-fetal medicine, pediatric surgery, and neonatology.  
- **Delivery planning** at a tertiary center ensures readiness for immediate respiratory support or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) if needed.

---

### **Summary Table**

| **Finding** | **Next Step** | **Rationale** |
|--------------|---------------|----------------|
| Isolated small CPAM | Ultrasound follow-up | >90% remain asymptomatic; low risk of hydrops |
| CPAM + other anomaly | Amniocentesis | Rule out chromosomal disorder |
| Signs of heart failure/hydrops | Fetal echocardiography | Evaluate cardiac function |
| Large lesion, unclear anatomy | Fetal MRI | Differentiate from CDH; estimate lung volume |
| Microcystic lesion with hydrops | Maternal steroids | May reduce lesion size |
