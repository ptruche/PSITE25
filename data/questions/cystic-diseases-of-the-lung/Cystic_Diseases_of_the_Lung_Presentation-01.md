A 20-week gestational ultrasound identifies a small echogenic mass in the right lower lobe of a fetus without mediastinal shift or hydrops. After delivery, the newborn is asymptomatic and discharged home after 48 hours. Which of the following best characterizes the presentation of congenital lung malformations such as CPAM and BPS?

<!-- EXPLANATION -->

**Congenital lung malformations (CLMs)**, including **congenital pulmonary airway malformation (CPAM)** and **bronchopulmonary sequestration (BPS)**, are increasingly diagnosed **prenatally** due to advances in **high-resolution fetal ultrasonography**. Most are **asymptomatic at birth**, though a subset may cause **respiratory distress** depending on size, location, and mass effect.

---

### **1) Prenatal Diagnosis**
- The widespread use of **second-trimester anatomic surveys (18–20 weeks gestation)** has dramatically increased detection rates.  
- Most lesions are identified **incidentally** during ultrasound and are **nonhydropic** at diagnosis.  
- **CPAM** and **BPS** constitute the **majority of antenatally diagnosed CLMs**.  
- Prognostic indicators, such as **mass-to-thorax ratio (CVR)** and **presence of hydrops**, predict the likelihood of fetal compromise.

| **Lesion** | **Timing of Detection** | **Typical Ultrasound Finding** |
|-------------|--------------------------|--------------------------------|
| CPAM | 18–22 weeks | Multicystic or echogenic lung mass |
| BPS | 18–22 weeks | Echogenic mass with systemic feeding vessel |
| CLE | Postnatal | Hyperinflation of single lobe after birth |

---

### **2) Postnatal Presentation**
- **Most infants with small lesions are asymptomatic** and can be **discharged within 48 hours** after delivery.  
- **Symptomatic neonates** typically present with:  
  - Respiratory distress due to large lesions or mass effect.  
  - Cyanosis or tachypnea from air trapping (CLE) or mediastinal shift.  
- **Older children** may present with **recurrent pneumonia** or chronic cough if undiagnosed.

| **Age at Presentation** | **Common Symptoms** |
|--------------------------|--------------------|
| **Neonatal** | Respiratory distress, tachypnea, cyanosis |
| **Infancy / Childhood** | Recurrent pneumonia, chronic cough |
| **Adolescence** | Rare; incidental imaging finding |

---

### **3) Prognosis and Risk Stratification**
- Most **CPAM and BPS lesions remain stable or regress** in utero.  
- **Prognostic factors** for poor outcome include:  
  - Large lesion size (CVR > 1.6)  
  - Hydrops fetalis  
  - Significant mediastinal shift or polyhydramnios  
- **Asymptomatic lesions** are often **electively resected** in infancy to prevent infection or rare malignant transformation.

---

### **4) Clinical Management**
- **Prenatal:** serial ultrasound or MRI monitoring; intervention (e.g., thoracoamniotic shunt) for large or hydropic lesions.  
- **Postnatal:** CT scan to confirm diagnosis and anatomy.  
- **Surgical excision:** typically elective in asymptomatic infants once stabilized.

---

**Key Points:**  
- Majority detected **prenatally**.  
- **CPAM** is the most common lesion (~60% of cases).  
- **Hybrid lesions (CPAM + BPS)** represent ~25%.  
- **Isolated BPS** accounts for 5–8%.  
- **CLE** rarely diagnosed in utero; usually postnatal due to air trapping.  

---

**Incorrect answers:**  
- **A:** Most lesions are asymptomatic and do not require emergent surgery.  
- **B:** Prenatal ultrasound frequently detects these lesions.  
- **D:** Only a minority develop hydrops or require intervention.  
- **E:** Hemoptysis is not a typical presenting symptom.  

**Correct answer: C — Most congenital pulmonary airway malformations (CPAM) and bronchopulmonary sequestrations (BPS) are now diagnosed incidentally on prenatal ultrasound and are asymptomatic at birth.**
