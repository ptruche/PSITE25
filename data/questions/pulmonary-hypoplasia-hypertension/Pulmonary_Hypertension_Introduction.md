A 3-month-old infant with congenital diaphragmatic hernia repair continues to have difficulty weaning from ventilator support.  Echocardiography demonstrates right-to-left shunting across a patent foramen ovale and an estimated right ventricular systolic pressure equal to 60 % of systemic systolic pressure.  Which of the following statements best characterizes this patient’s condition?

<!-- EXPLANATION -->

**Pulmonary hypertension (PH)** is a **pathophysiologic state of elevated pulmonary vascular pressure** that leads to **impaired pulmonary blood flow**, **right-ventricular (RV) strain**, and **abnormal gas exchange and hemodynamics**.  

### Definition
PH is defined as a **resting mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) ≥ 25 mm Hg**, measured by right-heart catheterization.  While this invasive measurement remains the **gold standard** for diagnosis and quantification of **pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR)**, it is rarely performed in neonates and infants unless diagnostic uncertainty exists.

### Functional Assessment
Because invasive testing is limited in critically ill children, **echocardiography** serves as the **principal screening and surveillance tool**.  Doppler-derived data can estimate RV systolic pressure via:
- **Tricuspid regurgitant jet velocity**, using the modified Bernoulli equation.  
- **Interventricular septal position and flattening**, which reflect pressure overload.  
- **Right-to-left shunting** through a **patent foramen ovale (PFO)** or **ductus arteriosus (PDA)**, which indicates suprasystemic or near-systemic RV pressure.  

The presence of these findings—particularly **RV pressure ≥ 50 % of systemic pressure**—is considered **clinically significant PH**.

### Pathophysiology
In neonates, pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) normally falls rapidly after birth.  Conditions that **impede or reverse this transition** (e.g., **congenital diaphragmatic hernia, meconium aspiration, sepsis, hypoxia**) maintain high PVR, causing **persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN)**.  Elevated afterload results in **RV hypertrophy and dilation**, leading to reduced pulmonary perfusion and systemic hypoxemia.

| **Measurement / Indicator** | **Normal / Mild PH** | **Clinically Significant PH** |
|------------------------------|----------------------|-------------------------------|
| Mean PA pressure | < 25 mm Hg | ≥ 25 mm Hg |
| RV pressure (echo estimate) | < 50 % systemic | ≥ 50 % systemic |
| Shunting across PFO / PDA | Left → right | **Right → left** |

### Clinical Correlates
- **Hypoxemia disproportionate to parenchymal disease**  
- **Pre- and post-ductal SpO₂ gradient**  
- **Loud second heart sound or tricuspid regurgitation murmur**  

### Diagnostic Strategy
1. **Echocardiography:** first-line, noninvasive, repeatable.  
2. **Cardiac catheterization:** definitive when needed to calculate **transpulmonary gradient = (mPAP – left atrial pressure) / pulmonary blood flow**.  
3. **Serial monitoring:** evaluates treatment response and guides oxygen, nitric oxide, or sildenafil therapy.

### Key Take-Home Concepts
- PH represents **pressure overload** of the right heart and **decreased pulmonary perfusion**.  
- mPAP ≥ 25 mm Hg defines disease; **echo evidence of RV pressure ≥ 50 % systemic** indicates clinically relevant PH.  
- **Echocardiography** is central for **diagnosis and surveillance**.  
- **Right-to-left shunting** signals significant PH, not its exclusion.

**Incorrect answers:**  
- **A:** Threshold is ≥ 25 mm Hg, not 20 mm Hg.  
- **B:** While catheterization quantifies PVR, **echo** is the mainstay for ongoing evaluation.  
- **D:** Right-to-left shunting confirms, not rules out, PH.  
- **E:** RV pressures **< 50 % systemic** suggest mild or absent PH.  

**Correct answer: C — Pulmonary hypertension is defined as mean PA pressure ≥ 25 mm Hg, and echocardiography is the principal noninvasive method for diagnosis and surveillance.**
